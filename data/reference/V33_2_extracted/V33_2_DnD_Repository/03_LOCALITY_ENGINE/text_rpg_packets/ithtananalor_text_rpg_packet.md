# Ithtananalor — Text RPG Flavor Packet

**Parent polity:** Independent (historically contested; current administration Sheresh-adjacent)
**Type:** Ancient settlement — archival site, old institutional memory, restricted access zones
**Truth layer:** conservative_inference

## Atmosphere

Ithtananalor is older than the current political boundaries and older than most of the institutions that would claim it, and this age is the primary fact about the place. The original structures — heavy-walled, low-windowed, built with a joinery technique that has not been in common use for two centuries — still form the settlement's core, and everything built since has been arranged around them rather than in their place. The newer construction is visible from the way the streets bend to accommodate old foundations and the way the rooflines change where one era meets another.

The settlement is not large and not prosperous in a commercial sense. What it has is records: physical archives going back to periods before the current polity structure, maintained in the old structures' controlled-temperature storage rooms by a staff of archivists who consider themselves custodians of something too important to be managed by whoever currently holds regional authority. The archives' independence is partly institutional memory and partly practical — no single polity has been able to claim clear succession to the founding body that established the archive, so the question of who owns the records has remained technically open, which means no one has been able to order their destruction.

## Scene Openers

1. **Dawn, the archive intake corridor.** An archivist is processing a new donation — a collection of documents from a dissolved merchant house whose surviving partner is legally required to deposit business records with a recognized archive. The archivist is cataloguing as he goes, and he has found three documents in the collection that predate the merchant house itself, which means they were acquired, not generated. He is setting those aside for a separate review.
2. **Midmorning, the old structures' outer corridor.** Two residents of the settlement are discussing the latest notice from the current regional administration, which wants to conduct a survey of the old structures for structural assessment purposes. The residents know that a structural assessment is a prelude to a jurisdiction claim, and they are discussing whether to allow the survey or to request that the archive's advisory board handle the response.
3. **Afternoon, the controlled-temperature reading room.** A visiting scholar is reviewing documents that took three weeks to access through the proper request process. She has two days of scheduled access. She is reading efficiently but she will not finish in two days, which means she must apply for an extension, which means another review process. The chief archivist who processes extension requests is visible through the reading room window, working at his own desk.
4. **Evening, the settlement's outer edge.** The handful of non-archive residents — the service workers, the tavern keeper, the supplies factor — are present at the edge of the settlement in the way that people who support a larger institution tend to be present: necessary, competent, and socially distinct from the people whose work the institution is actually for. The tavern keeper knows more about the archive staff than the staff knows about her.

## Social Register

- The archive's access request process is not negotiable and not expeditable through personal relationship. The process exists specifically to prevent the archive from being used selectively, and the archivists enforce it on themselves as much as on visitors. Asking an archivist to informally review something before a formal request is complete is a question they will decline without reproach.
- Records donated to the archive are legally the archive's property. The donor retains a right of consultation but not of retrieval or copy except through the standard access process. A donor who donated records and later wants them back is informed of this, once, clearly.
- The settlement's residents distinguish between archive staff and visiting scholars not by how they dress but by how they move — staff move in the old structures without hesitation; scholars move with the attention of people who are not sure what they are allowed to touch. Locals read this immediately.
- The question of which polity has authority over Ithtananalor is not discussed by residents in the presence of unfamiliar visitors. It is a chronic source of administrative friction and a topic that has caused difficulty in the past. The settlement's residents have learned to redirect rather than engage.
- The old structures' restricted zones are marked with a system developed before the current signage standard, which means the marks look different from what visitors expect. The archivists explain this to new visitors exactly once.

## Signature Details

- **The joinery of the old structures.** The original buildings use a beam-and-peg construction technique that interlocks without nails or mortar. The joints flex with temperature changes rather than resisting them, which is why the structures have survived when more rigid construction in the same era has not. The archivists can identify which beams are original and which have been replaced, and they can date the replacements by the technique used.
- **The access request ledger.** Every request to review archive materials is entered in a physical ledger before it is processed — the ledger predates any digital or administrative system and is considered the primary record of access. A scholar who has used the archive before will have a number in the ledger. A new scholar does not have a number until their first request is entered.
- **The temperature difference.** The old structures maintain a different temperature than the settlement around them — cooler in summer, warmer in winter, by a consistent margin that the archivists attribute to the wall thickness and the orientation relative to the prevailing wind. Visitors who have been in the reading room for more than an hour step outside and feel the difference immediately.

## Complication Seed

A set of documents requested by a visiting scholar two months ago was prepared for her access but never retrieved — she canceled her visit without notification and has not since contacted the archive. The documents are sitting in the reading room preparation area in a state they cannot remain in indefinitely: prepared documents must either be accessed and returned to storage or returned to storage directly, and the process for returning uncollected prepared documents requires a notation in the access ledger that the archivist responsible for preparation finds procedurally uncomfortable. The notation implies the scholar abandoned the request, which has administrative implications the archivist has not determined and is reluctant to trigger. He is looking for guidance that does not come from within the archive, because the guidance from within the archive is to follow the process.
