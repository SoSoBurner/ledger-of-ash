# Panim Haven — Text RPG Flavor Packet

**Parent polity:** Panim
**Type:** Haven settlement — coastal refuge, provisioning point, passage staging
**Truth layer:** conservative_inference

## Atmosphere

The Haven earns its name by function: it is where ships shelter when the coastal conditions on the Panim stretch turn severe, where inland travelers stage before committing to the longer coastal route, where people who need to stop without being too visible tend to stop. The settlement is built for temporary occupancy in a way that looks permanent — the structures are solid but not elaborate, the provisioning facilities are comprehensive but not specialized, and the inn keeps more rooms than the current population would usually fill because the current population shifts with the weather and the route conditions.

The water here is darker than the open coast — a function of the bay's depth and the particular mineral content of the inlet floor — and the boats in the harbor have a quality of patient waiting about them that distinguishes vessels wintering over from vessels that arrived yesterday. The long-term harbor community has its own social world entirely separate from the passing travelers, and the two groups occupy the same settlement without occupying the same space.

## Scene Openers

1. **Dawn, the harbor mouth.** A weather observation is being conducted by the harbor master's assistant, who reads the inlet conditions each morning before the harbor record is updated. Today's conditions are borderline for the outer coastal route — navigable but not comfortable — and the assistant will record it as navigable, which means the vessels currently waiting for a clear window will be expected to move if they intend to. Two of the three captains waiting know the assessment will come out this way and have already decided they're not moving regardless.
2. **Midmorning, the provisioning house.** A factor is assembling a supply order for a vessel that needs to be stocked for a longer passage than originally planned. The delay has changed the route requirements. She is recalculating costs against the vessel's remaining budget, and the calculation is close. The provisioning house manager is watching her work and already knows the conclusion she will reach.
3. **Afternoon, the inn's common room.** The room contains travelers at four different stages of transit: people waiting for conditions to clear, people who arrived last night and are deciding whether to continue, people who have been here three days and have started to feel like residents, and one man who has been here two weeks and whose stated reason for staying changes every time someone asks. The inn keeper knows which category each person is in.
4. **Evening, the harbor community's gathering area.** The long-term harbor residents — the boat maintenance crews, the inlet pilots, the weather readers — have their own space at the edge of the harbor district that passing travelers learn not to enter without an introduction. The social rules there are entirely informal and entirely enforced.

## Social Register

- The harbor record is the official determination of route conditions. A vessel that chooses not to move on a navigable-assessment day is not in violation of any rule, but it is noted in the record and becomes part of the vessel's standing in the harbor community's informal assessment of its captain.
- The provisioning house does not extend credit to vessels it does not have history with. First-time customers pay at the time of order. Long-term customers have accounts. The difference is made visible by the provisioning manager at the intake desk, without comment.
- The two-week resident at the inn is a known quantity to the inn keeper and to the long-term harbor community, neither of whom will discuss him with strangers. The harbor community's position is that his presence is his own business as long as he creates no problems. The inn keeper's position is the same.
- Asking the harbor master's office about another vessel's schedule, arrival time, or cargo is a question that will be redirected to the vessel's captain directly. The harbor office tracks vessel movements for administrative purposes and does not share information between unrelated parties.
- Weather assessments from the harbor record are public but their interpretation is contested. Everyone in the Haven has a view about whether the assessment is conservative or accurate, and the long-term residents have earned the right to express that view. Visitors who express the same view before they have earned it will be corrected.

## Signature Details

- **The dark water.** The inlet's mineral content turns the water a dark gray-green distinct from the coastal blue visible at the harbor mouth. Long-term residents and returning captains orient themselves in the harbor by color without thinking about it. New arrivals tend to comment on it. The local response is a nod that does not invite elaboration.
- **The harbor record board.** A physical board at the harbor master's office entrance posts the day's condition assessment, the vessels currently registered as in harbor, and any notices relevant to the current route conditions. The board is updated at dawn and after any significant weather change. Locals check it as a matter of course; visitors who do not check it are making an assumption the Haven's culture considers unwise.
- **The long-residence markers.** Vessels that have been in harbor for more than ten days are marked with a secondary flag — a harbor convention that distinguishes extended stays from transit. The marker is not an official designation but it is universally recognized, and long-term residents of the harbor community have developed opinions about the vessels that hold it and the reasons they are staying.

## Complication Seed

A vessel has been in harbor for seventeen days under the long-residence flag. Its captain has given the harbor master's office three different explanations for the extended stay — weather, mechanical, crew illness — no one of which is untrue but none of which fully accounts for seventeen days. The harbor community is aware of the vessel's presence and has reached its own private conclusion about why it is staying, which they do not share with passing travelers. A factor who did provisioning for the vessel on day three has noted that the supply order was for a longer passage than the stated mechanical delay would require, and she is sitting on this observation because it is not her business — unless someone she trusts asks her a question that makes it her business.
