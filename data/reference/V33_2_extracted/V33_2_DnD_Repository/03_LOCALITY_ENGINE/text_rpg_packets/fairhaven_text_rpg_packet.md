# Fairhaven — Text RPG Flavor Packet

**Parent polity:** Principality of Shelk (peripheral)
**Type:** Market town — agricultural processing, route intersection, seasonal trade
**Truth layer:** conservative_inference

## Atmosphere

Fairhaven sits at the intersection of two agricultural routes, and in the right season it smells of grain and rendered fat from the processing facilities at the south end of the market square. The town is not large but it is consistently occupied — the farmers who bring their loads in, the factors who buy from them, the carriers who move the processed goods on, the service workers who support all of them. The population turns over on a rhythm tied to the growing season, which means Fairhaven always has a stratum of long-term residents and a stratum of seasonal people, and the two strata know themselves apart without needing to discuss it.

The architecture is functional and well-maintained in the way of a place that handles freight daily — loading platforms built to the right height, storage doors wide enough for carts, the market square paved in a material chosen for drainage rather than appearance. There is a small civic building on the square's north side where the route tolls are collected and the trade records kept, and the person who runs it — currently a middle-aged man who has been in the position for eight years — knows more about what moves through Fairhaven than anyone else.

## Scene Openers

1. **Dawn, the south processing facility.** The overnight grain run has come in and the facility is processing — milling, sorting, sacking — in the first light. The workers are at the third hour of a shift that started before dawn, and the rhythm is established and efficient. A new load has just arrived and the factor who brought it is waiting at the intake window, watching the existing load move, calculating whether his timing was right.
2. **Midmorning, the market square.** Three farmers are comparing prices on their current load against what they were offered last week. Two of them think the week's price is fair; one thinks it is being suppressed, and he has a theory about which buyer is responsible. The theory may be correct. The other two are listening with the skepticism of people who have heard theories before.
3. **Afternoon, the toll collection office.** A carrier is presenting his route documentation for the current load. The toll collector is processing it with the attention of someone who has learned that errors discovered later are more expensive than corrections made now. He notices a route description that should read east but reads west. He flags it. The carrier sighs. This is the third time this quarter.
4. **Evening, the market-side tavern.** The seasonal workers and the long-term residents do not sit at different tables by rule, but they tend to. The seasonal workers are louder because they are further from home and have less to lose by being noticed. The long-term residents are quieter and track more of what is said. The tavern keeper is long-term.

## Social Register

- The processing facilities operate on intake order, not on social standing or purchase volume. A large buyer who arrives after a small one waits. This is enforced by the facility staff and is not a negotiable point.
- The toll office records are public in principle — the record of what passed through Fairhaven is a public ledger — but requesting access to specific entries requires a stated purpose. The toll collector will provide access readily for reasons he finds legitimate and will remember requests he found unusual.
- Pricing discussions happen at the square and they are expected to be conducted in earshot of the relevant parties. Private price negotiations between buyers and major sellers that exclude competing buyers are not prohibited, but they generate resentment that accumulates across seasons.
- The long-term residents have a social hierarchy that is entirely invisible to seasonal visitors and entirely legible to each other. Treating a long-term resident as a seasonal worker — or a seasonal worker as having local standing they haven't earned — creates friction that the tavern keeper and the toll collector will both notice.
- Fairhaven's seasonal rhythm means that some social customs shift between growing season and off-season. The town is more formal when it is full and more direct when it is not. Arriving in the off-season and using the growing-season register reads as either affectation or unfamiliarity.

## Signature Details

- **The intake window queue.** The south facility's intake window has a painted queue marker on the ground — a set of parallel lines indicating where carts should line up to present their load. The marker is a social contract as much as an organizational tool, and a driver who ignores it and pulls forward is pulled back by peer pressure before the facility staff need to intervene.
- **The toll ledger.** The eight-year toll collector's handwriting is known to the regular carriers and factors, and they have learned to read his notation system, which records not just the toll amount but the condition of the route documentation. A neat notation means clean passage. A notation with a secondary mark means something was flagged.
- **The grain smell.** Fairhaven smells of processed grain and rendered fat throughout the growing season and into the early off-season, when the residue from the processing year takes time to dissipate. Long-term residents do not notice it. Arriving visitors do, and the way they register it — or don't — is one of the tells that separates people who know the town from people who don't.

## Complication Seed

The toll ledger from two seasons back has an entry that a factor has recently identified as inconsistent with her own records from the same period. The inconsistency is small — a single load, a difference in the recorded weight — but the factor believes it represents a systematic error rather than a one-off, because she found a similar discrepancy in an entry from three seasons ago. She has not brought this to the toll collector directly because the toll collector is the only person who could have made the entries, and she is uncertain whether she is looking at honest error or deliberate adjustment. She is looking for someone to review the ledger entries from both periods against independent documentation — freight manifests held by the carriers who ran the loads — without revealing what she suspects, because if she is wrong, the accusation damages a relationship she depends on.
